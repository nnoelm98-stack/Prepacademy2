Scatterplots & Data Analysis
Master reading and interpreting scatterplots, lines of best fit, and two-variable data — and learn the critical distinction between correlation and causation that the SAT tests every year.
12 min read3 practice questions+20 pts on completion
Scatterplot concepts
Positive association
As x increases, y tends to increase. Points slope upward from left to right. Line of best fit has positive slope.
Study time vs. test score
Height vs. weight
Negative association
As x increases, y tends to decrease. Points slope downward from left to right. Line of best fit has negative slope.
Hours of TV vs. grades
Distance from city vs. property value
Line of best fit
A line drawn through a scatterplot that minimizes the overall distance to all data points. Used to make predictions and estimate values not in the dataset.
Read it like any linear equation: slope = rate of change, y-intercept = predicted y when x = 0.
Interpolation vs. extrapolation
Interpolation = predicting within the range of the data (reliable). Extrapolation = predicting outside the data range (less reliable — the SAT may ask about this).
Data from x=10 to x=50. Predicting at x=30 = interpolation. Predicting at x=100 = extrapolation.
Strength of association
Strong association: points cluster closely around the line. Weak association: points are scattered far from the line. No association: no clear pattern.
Outliers in scatterplots
A point far from the line of best fit. Outliers don't necessarily invalidate the trend — they may represent unusual cases worth investigating separately.
Correlation ≠ Causation — the SAT's most tested statistical concept
A scatterplot showing a strong association between two variables does NOT mean one causes the other. Both variables might be caused by a third factor (confounding variable), or the association might be coincidental. The SAT will always present incorrect answer choices that claim causation from correlation data.
SAT strategy tips
Read the line of best fit as an equation
When given a line of best fit, identify the slope and y-intercept. The slope tells you the predicted change in y per unit increase in x. Use the equation to make predictions at specific x-values.
Correlation ≠ causation — always
If an answer choice says "X causes Y" based on a scatterplot, it's wrong. Scatterplots show association, not causation. The SAT always includes "X causes Y" as a trap answer.
Residuals = actual − predicted
A residual is the difference between an actual data point and the value predicted by the line of best fit. Positive residual = above the line. Negative residual = below the line.
Two-way tables — marginal vs. conditional
In two-way tables, "marginal" totals are the row/column sums. "Conditional" probability uses a specific row or column as the denominator. Read the question carefully to know which total to use as the base.
Practice questions
Problem
A scatterplot shows the relationship between hours spent studying (x) and exam score (y). The line of best fit has the equation:
y = 4.5x + 42
The slope 4.5 means for each additional hour of study, the predicted score increases by 4.5 points. The y-intercept 42 is the predicted score with 0 hours of study.
Question 1 · Interpreting Line of Best Fit
What does the slope of this line of best fit represent?
AThe predicted exam score for a student who studies 0 hours
BThe predicted increase in exam score for each additional hour of study
CThe total number of hours studied by all students
DThe maximum possible exam score
Correct! The slope (4.5) represents the rate of change — for each additional hour of study (x increases by 1), the predicted score (y) increases by 4.5 points. Option A describes the y-intercept (42), not the slope.
Not quite. In y = mx + b, the slope m tells you how much y changes per unit increase in x. Here, for every 1 additional hour studied, what happens to the predicted score?
slope = 4.5 → score increases 4.5 points per hour
Problem
A researcher finds a strong positive association between the number of fire trucks sent to a fire and the total damage caused by the fire.
This is a correlation vs. causation question. A strong association does NOT mean one variable causes the other. Ask: is there a third variable that explains both?
Question 2 · Correlation vs. Causation
Which conclusion is best supported by this finding?
ASending more fire trucks causes more damage to the fire.
BThe number of fire trucks and damage are positively associated, likely because larger fires require both more trucks and cause more damage.
CCities should send fewer fire trucks to reduce fire damage.
DFire damage causes cities to buy more fire trucks.
Correct! A positive association between trucks and damage doesn't mean trucks cause damage. The confounding variable is fire size — larger fires require more trucks AND cause more damage. Both variables are driven by a third factor (fire size). Options A and C wrongly imply causation and would lead to absurd policy conclusions. Option D reverses the logic entirely.
Not quite. Correlation does NOT imply causation. Think about what else might explain why bigger fires have both more trucks and more damage. Is there a third variable driving both?
Fire size → more trucks AND more damage. Trucks don't cause damage.
Problem
The line of best fit for a scatterplot has equation y = 2x + 5. One data point in the scatterplot is (6, 20).
The residual = actual y − predicted y. First find the predicted y from the line of best fit at x = 6. Then subtract from the actual y value of 20.
Question 3 · Residuals
What is the residual for the point (6, 20)?
A−3
B3
C7
D−7
Correct! Residual = 3
Predicted y at x=6: y = 2(6)+5 = 17
Actual y = 20
Residual = actual − predicted = 20 − 17 = 3 ✓

Positive residual → point is ABOVE the line of best fit.
The actual score (20) is 3 higher than the model predicts (17).
Not quite. Residual = actual y − predicted y. Find the predicted value using the equation: y = 2(6) + 5 = 17. Then subtract from the actual value of 20.
20 − 17 = 3 (point is above the line)
