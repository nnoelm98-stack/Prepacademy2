Statistics & Data Analysis
Go deeper on statistics — sampling methods, margin of error, study design, and drawing valid conclusions from data — the critical reasoning layer the SAT adds on top of basic statistics.
13 min read3 practice questions+20 pts on completion
Beyond the numbers — study design and inference
The SAT doesn't just ask you to calculate statistics. It asks whether conclusions drawn from a study are valid. This requires understanding how data was collected and what it can and cannot tell us.
Random sampling
A random sample allows generalizations to the population it was drawn from. Non-random samples (convenience, voluntary) may be biased — results may not generalize.
Random survey of 500 voters → can generalize to all voters. Survey only online → biased toward internet users.
Observational study vs. experiment
Observational studies can show association but NOT causation. Only randomized controlled experiments can establish causation.
Observational: people who eat breakfast earn more. Cannot say breakfast causes higher earnings — confounding variables exist.
Margin of error
Survey results come with uncertainty: reported value ± margin of error. If two groups' intervals overlap, you cannot conclude they are different.
Group A: 52% ± 4% → (48%, 56%)
Group B: 49% ± 4% → (45%, 53%)
Intervals overlap → can't say A > B.
Sample size and confidence
Larger samples reduce margin of error and produce more reliable estimates. A study with n=50 is less reliable than n=5,000, all else being equal.
Confounding variables
A lurking variable that affects both the explanatory and response variables, creating a spurious association. The SAT asks you to identify confounding variables when evaluating study conclusions.
Generalizing to population
Only random samples allow generalization. The conclusion must match the scope of the sample — you can only generalize to the population from which the sample was drawn.
SAT strategy tips
Association ≠ causation (again)
Even in this lesson — any conclusion saying "X causes Y" from an observational study is wrong. The SAT will always have this as a trap answer. Only a randomized experiment can establish causation.
Scope of conclusion must match sample
If a study surveys students at one high school, conclusions can only apply to students at that school — not all students nationwide. "Overgeneralizing" is always a wrong answer.
Overlapping margin of error intervals
If two confidence intervals overlap, you cannot conclude one group is higher than the other. The SAT frequently tests this — look for "the data support the conclusion that Group A scored higher" when intervals overlap.
Random assignment eliminates confounding
In a randomized experiment, participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control. This balances confounding variables and allows causal conclusions — it's the key distinction between experiments and observational studies.
Practice questions
Problem
Researchers randomly selected 800 adults from a city and found that 68% reported exercising at least three times per week. The margin of error was ±3.5%.
Margin of error creates a confidence interval: 68% ± 3.5% = (64.5%, 71.5%). Conclusions that fall within this range are supported; conclusions outside are not supported.
Question 1 · Margin of Error
Which conclusion is best supported by this data?
AExactly 68% of all adults in the city exercise at least three times per week.
BIt is plausible that between 64.5% and 71.5% of adults in the city exercise at least three times per week.
CMore than 75% of adults in the city exercise at least three times per week.
DThese results apply to all adults in the country.
Correct! Option B correctly states the confidence interval: 68% ± 3.5% = 64.5% to 71.5%. This is the appropriate conclusion from a survey with a margin of error. Option A claims exactness — surveys never give exact values. Option C (75%) is outside the confidence interval. Option D overgeneralizes — the sample was from one city, not the country.
Not quite. The margin of error means the true value is likely 68% ± 3.5% = between 64.5% and 71.5%. Which conclusion stays within this range and uses appropriately uncertain language?
Problem
A school randomly assigned 150 students to either a new study skills program or a control group. After 6 weeks, students in the program scored an average of 12 points higher on tests than the control group.
Key question: was this an observational study or a randomized experiment? Random assignment is the key phrase. Does random assignment allow causal conclusions?
Question 2 · Study Design
Which conclusion is most justified by this study?
AStudents who study more naturally score higher, which explains the difference.
BThe study skills program caused an improvement in test scores among the students in this study.
CAll students in the country would improve by 12 points if given this program.
DThere is an association between the program and test scores, but causation cannot be determined.
Correct! Because students were randomly assigned (not self-selected), this is a randomized controlled experiment — the gold standard for causal inference. Option B correctly identifies a causal conclusion limited to the study participants. Option C overgeneralizes. Option D is wrong — random assignment DOES allow causal conclusions. Option A invents an explanation not in the study.
Not quite. The key detail is "randomly assigned." Random assignment creates comparable groups and eliminates confounding — it's what makes causal conclusions possible in experiments. What scope is appropriate for this conclusion?
Problem
A survey of 200 randomly selected customers at a coffee shop found that 74% preferred oat milk over regular milk. The margin of error was ±6%.
A separate survey of 200 randomly selected customers at the same coffee shop the following week found that 68% preferred oat milk. The margin of error was also ±6%.
Week 1 interval: 74% ± 6% = (68%, 80%). Week 2 interval: 68% ± 6% = (62%, 74%). Do these intervals overlap?
Question 3 · Overlapping Intervals
Which statement is best supported by the two surveys?
AOat milk preference significantly declined between the two weeks.
BThe difference in results between the two weeks is statistically meaningful.
CThe two surveys are consistent — the observed difference is within the range of sampling variability.
DWeek 1 preference was definitely higher than Week 2.
Correct!
Week 1 interval: 68% to 80%
Week 2 interval: 62% to 74%
Overlap region: 68% to 74%

The intervals overlap significantly → the difference (74% vs 68%) is within sampling variability. We cannot conclude there was a real change. Options A, B, and D all claim the difference is meaningful — but with overlapping intervals, this cannot be concluded.
Not quite. Calculate each confidence interval: Week 1 = 68%–80%, Week 2 = 62%–74%. Do they overlap? If yes, the difference is within sampling variability — we cannot conclude a real change occurred.
Overlap: 68%–74% → consistent, not a significant change
