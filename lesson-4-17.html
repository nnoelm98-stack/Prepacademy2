Inference & Sampling
Master the logic of statistical inference — making population estimates from samples — and learn to evaluate the validity and scope of conclusions drawn from survey and experimental data.
12 min read3 practice questions+20 pts on completion
From sample to population — the inference chain
Statistical inference lets us draw conclusions about a population based on a sample. The validity of these conclusions depends entirely on how the sample was selected.
Simple random sample (SRS)
Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Results can be generalized to the population. The gold standard for surveys.
Voluntary response bias
When people self-select into a survey (calling a hotline, clicking an online poll), strong opinions are over-represented. Results cannot be generalized.
Online polls, call-in surveys → voluntary response bias
Convenience sample
Selecting whoever is easiest to reach (students in one class, people at a mall). Not representative — can't generalize to the broader population.
Undercoverage bias
When some groups in the population are less likely to be included in the sample than others. The sample systematically misses certain subgroups.
Scope of inference — generalizability
You can only generalize results to the population from which the sample was randomly drawn. Broader claims beyond that population are not supported.
Random sample of one school → can generalize to that school only, not all schools.
Estimation from sample to population
If p% of a random sample has a property, we estimate p% of the population has it. This estimate applies only to the population the sample came from.
40% of a random sample of 500 city residents → estimate 40% of all city residents.
The most common SAT inference traps
Trap 1: Claiming the sample result is exactly equal to the population parameter (ignoring sampling variability). Trap 2: Generalizing beyond the population sampled (e.g., from one city to the whole country). Trap 3: Drawing causal conclusions from an observational study. Trap 4: Concluding causation from a biased sample.
SAT strategy tips
Estimate counts from proportions
If 35% of a random sample of 200 prefer option A, and the city population is 50,000, estimate: 0.35 × 50,000 = 17,500 city residents prefer option A. This proportional extrapolation is valid only for random samples.
Always check how the sample was selected
Before evaluating any conclusion, identify the sampling method. Was it random? Who had a chance to respond? Non-random or voluntary samples cannot support population-wide conclusions — the SAT will test this every time.
Generalize only to the sampled population
If a study samples customers of one store, conclusions apply to customers of that store — not to all shoppers nationwide. The scope of conclusion must match the scope of sampling. Over-generalizing is always a wrong answer.
Larger sample → smaller margin of error
All else equal, larger samples produce more reliable estimates with narrower confidence intervals. The SAT may ask which of two study designs produces more reliable estimates — choose the one with the larger random sample.
Practice questions
Problem
A random sample of 400 residents from a city of 120,000 was surveyed. 28% said they use public transportation daily.
For a random sample, you can estimate the population proportion. Apply 28% to the city's total population of 120,000: 0.28 × 120,000 = 33,600.
Question 1 · Population Estimate
Which is the best estimate of the number of city residents who use public transportation daily?
A112 residents
B28,000 residents
C33,600 residents
D400 residents
Correct! 33,600 residents
Random sample → can estimate population proportion
Estimated count = 28% × 120,000 = 0.28 × 120,000 = 33,600 ✓

Problem
A researcher posts an online survey asking people whether they support a new local park. Of the 250 respondents, 82% said yes.
This is a voluntary response sample — only people who chose to respond participated. Online surveys are subject to voluntary response bias. Who is most likely to click and respond?
Question 2 · Sampling Bias
Which limitation most affects this survey's conclusions?
AThe sample size of 250 is too small to draw any conclusions.
BVoluntary response bias — people with strong opinions (especially supporters) are more likely to respond, over-representing support for the park.
CThe 82% result is too high to be credible.
DThe survey should have asked more questions to get accurate results.
Correct! An online opt-in survey has voluntary response bias — the 250 people who chose to respond are not representative of all residents. People who strongly support the park are more likely to click and complete the survey, inflating the "yes" percentage. Option A is incorrect — 250 can be adequate for a random sample; the issue is how they were selected, not how many.
Not quite. The problem isn't sample size — it's how the sample was collected. What happens to an online survey where people self-select to participate?
Voluntary response → strong opinions over-represented → biased
Problem
Two schools each conducted a survey about students' sleep habits. School A used a random sample of 300 students; School B surveyed every student who attended a health class (120 students).
Compare the two sampling methods: School A is random (can generalize to all students at that school). School B is a convenience sample (health class students may have different sleep habits than average).
Question 3 · Comparing Survey Designs
Whose results can be more reliably generalized to their school's entire student population?
ASchool B, because 100% of health class students were surveyed.
BSchool A, because a random sample better represents the full student population.
CSchool A, because 300 is a larger sample than 120.
DNeither — neither study used a national random sample.
Correct! School A The reason is the random sampling method, not merely the larger size (B is also true but not the primary reason). School B's health class students are a convenience sample — health-conscious students may sleep differently from the average student, making the results unrepresentative. School A's random sample gives all students an equal chance of inclusion, so it best represents the full school. Option D is wrong — generalization to the school doesn't require a national sample.
