Geometry Applications
Apply all geometry skills to complex multi-step real-world problems — architectural design, surveying, packaging, and navigation — where multiple geometry concepts combine in a single question.
13 min read3 practice questions+20 pts on completion
Combining geometry concepts
The most challenging SAT geometry questions require combining multiple concepts — for example, using trigonometry inside a volume calculation, or applying similar triangles within a coordinate geometry problem. The key is identifying which tools are needed before starting any calculation.
Trig in 3D problems
Use trig ratios to find missing lengths in cross-sections of 3D figures. Find the height of a cone from a slant height and angle, or the diagonal of a box.
Box 8×6×h. Space diagonal d:
d = √(8²+6²+h²) = √(100+h²)
Scale and proportion in geometry
When dimensions scale by k: lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³. Common SAT question: a shape is scaled by factor k — how does the perimeter, area, or volume change?
Circles and triangles combined
Many problems combine a circle with an inscribed or circumscribed polygon. The radius connects to the polygon's vertices or sides. Draw the radius to a key point and look for right angles.
Equilateral triangle inscribed in circle of r=6:
Side = r√3 = 6√3
Coordinate geometry + distance
Find the distance between a point and a line, the area of a polygon from coordinates, or whether a point lies inside a circle. These combine formulas from different geometry topics.
Worked example — multi-step geometry
A right circular cone has a slant height of 13 cm and a base radius of 5 cm. Find the volume.

Step 1: Find height using Pythagorean theorem.
h² + r² = l² → h² + 25 = 169 → h² = 144 → h = 12 cm
Step 2: V = ⅓πr²h = ⅓π(25)(12) = 100π cm³
SAT strategy tips
Identify required concepts before calculating
Read the entire problem and list which geometry concepts are needed. Multi-step problems often require: (1) a trig ratio or Pythagorean theorem to find a length, then (2) that length in a volume or area formula. Doing step 2 before step 1 is the most common error.
Label every measurement on your diagram
Even in calculator section geometry, drawing and labeling a diagram is faster than holding dimensions in your head. Write all given measurements and intermediary results on the figure as you find them.
Slant height ≠ height in cones
The slant height l is the distance along the cone's surface from apex to base edge. The height h is the perpendicular distance from apex to base. They're related by h² + r² = l². Volume and surface area formulas use height h, not slant height l.
The space diagonal of a box uses all three dimensions
The longest diagonal through a rectangular box (from one corner to the opposite corner) equals √(l² + w² + h²). This is the Pythagorean theorem applied twice — first to find the face diagonal, then using that as a leg.

A storage box has dimensions 9 cm × 12 cm × 8 cm. A diagonal rod is placed inside the box, connecting opposite corners.
Space diagonal = √(l²+w²+h²) = √(9²+12²+8²) = √(81+144+64) = √289. Recognize: 289 = 17².

Problem
A point P is located at (3, 7) in the xy-plane. A circle has center (−1, 4) and radius 6.
Find the distance from P to the center using the distance formula. Then compare to the radius: if distance < r, P is inside; if distance = r, P is on; if distance > r, P is outside.
Question 2 · Point and Circle
Is point P inside, on, or outside the circle?
AInside the circle (distance < 6)
BOn the circle (distance = 6)
COutside the circle (distance > 6)
DCannot be determined without the equation
Correct! Inside the circle
Distance from P(3,7) to center (−1,4):
d = √[(3−(−1))² + (7−4)²]
= √[4² + 3²]
= √[16 + 9] = √25 = 5
Since 5 < 6 (radius), P is INSIDE the circle ✓
Not quite. Use the distance formula between P(3,7) and center(−1,4). Compare to r=6: less than → inside, equal → on, greater → outside.
d = √(16+9) = 5 < 6 → inside
Problem
A surveyor needs to find the width of a river. She stands at point A on one bank, directly across from point B on the other bank. She walks 80 m along the bank to point C. From C, the angle between line CB and line CA is 35°.
Triangle ABC: AB is the width (opposite the 35° angle at C), AC = 80 m (adjacent to the 35° angle). Use tan(35°) = AB/AC → AB = 80 × tan(35°). (tan 35° ≈ 0.700)
Question 3 · Trig Application

Not quite. The width AB is opposite the 35° angle. The 80m walk AC is adjacent. Use tangent: tan(35°) = opposite/adjacent = AB/80.
AB = 80 × tan(35°) ≈ 80 × 0.700 ≈ 56 m
