How the SAT mixes concepts
On the real SAT, the hardest questions require you to recognize which concept applies, translate a word problem into the right mathematical structure, and execute cleanly. Here are the most important cross-topic combinations tested.
Algebra + Geometry
Set up an equation from a geometric constraint (perimeter, area, angle sum), then solve the algebra. Or use coordinate geometry to find geometric properties.
Exponential + Statistics
Fit an exponential model to real data, then interpret the model's parameters in context. Or identify whether a table shows linear or exponential growth before modeling.
Proportions + Percent change
Multi-step problems that combine ratio setup with percentage calculations — markups, conversions, and rate comparisons all in one problem.
Systems + Inequalities
Real-world constraints written as a mixed system (some equations, some inequalities) requiring substitution or test-a-point strategies to find the valid region.
Functions + Transformations
Given a graph of f(x), identify what transformation produces g(x), or find specific values of a transformed function using points from the original.
Statistics + Inference
Calculate a statistic from a sample, then evaluate whether the conclusion drawn (generalization, causation, margin of error) is valid.
Final exam strategy
Read the question before the problem
On multi-step problems, reading the question first tells you what you're solving for. This focuses your attention and prevents you from calculating things you don't need.
Label variables, write equations, then calculate
Every word problem requires three steps: (1) define variables, (2) write equations or inequalities, (3) solve. Students who skip to calculation make avoidable errors. The calculator helps with step 3 only.
Verify your answer makes sense
After solving, ask: is the answer reasonable? A negative length, a probability over 1, or a population that shrank when it was supposed to grow — all signal an error. Budget 5 seconds to sanity-check every answer.
Don't get stuck — use process of elimination
If a question is taking too long, eliminate obviously wrong answers and make an educated guess. Then mark it and move on. On the digital SAT, every question has equal weight — don't spend 5 minutes on one question at the expense of three others.

Multi-concept problem
A square has side length s. The side is increased by 20%, creating a larger square.
New side = 1.2s. New area = (1.2s)² = 1.44s². Original area = s². Percent increase = (1.44s² − s²)/s² × 100 = 44%.
Question 1 · Geometry + Percentages
By what percent does the area of the square increase?
A20%
B40%
C44%
D24%
Correct! 44%
New side = 1.2s
New area = (1.2s)² = 1.44s²
Original area = s²
% increase = (1.44s² − s²)/s² × 100
= 0.44 × 100 = 44% ✓

Key insight: a 20% increase in length → 1.2² = 1.44 → 44% increase in area. Linear and area scales are different!
Not quite. Area scales as the square of the linear dimension. New side = 1.2s → new area = (1.2s)² = 1.44s². The area increases by 44%, not 20% × 2 = 40%.
(1.2)² = 1.44 → 44% area increase
Multi-concept problem
A researcher randomly surveyed 500 visitors to a national park. 35% said they visited the park more than 3 times per year. The park receives approximately 180,000 visitors per year.
The researcher concluded: "Visiting the park more than 3 times per year causes visitors to develop stronger environmental values."
This question combines inference (was the sample valid?) with study design (can an observational survey establish causation?). Identify both potential flaws.
Question 2 · Statistics + Inference
Which best identifies a flaw in the researcher's conclusion?
AThe sample size of 500 is too small to draw any conclusions.
BA survey cannot establish causation — frequent visitors may already hold stronger environmental values before visiting more.
CThe estimate of 35% cannot be applied to the full 180,000 visitors.
DThe survey should have been conducted online for more responses.
Correct! The fundamental flaw is that an observational survey cannot establish causation. The researcher observed an association between visit frequency and environmental values — but this doesn't mean visiting more caused the values. People who already have strong environmental values may choose to visit more. This is the classic correlation ≠ causation error. A (sample size) is wrong — 500 is a reasonable sample. C is wrong — a random sample can be used to estimate population proportions. D is irrelevant and would make the sample worse (voluntary response bias).
Not quite. The researcher used the word "causes" — but from an observational survey, can you establish causation? What alternative explanation might exist for the association?
Survey shows association, not causation. Confounding possible.
Multi-concept problem
A company's profit P (in thousands of dollars) after t years is modeled by:
P(t) = −2t² + 16t − 24
Maximum profit: find vertex at t = −16/(2×−2) = 4 years. Profit becomes positive when P(t) > 0: factor −2t²+16t−24 > 0 → −2(t²−8t+12) > 0 → −2(t−2)(t−6) > 0. Positive when 2 < t < 6.
Question 3 · Algebra + Interpretation
During which time interval is the company profitable (P > 0)?
At < 2 and t > 6
B2 < t < 6
C0 < t < 8
Dt > 4
Correct! 2 < t < 6
P(t) > 0: −2t²+16t−24 > 0
Divide by −2 (flip inequality): t²−8t+12 < 0
Factor: (t−2)(t−6) < 0
Negative between roots → 2 < t < 6 ✓

Verify: P(4) = −32+64−24 = 8 > 0 ✓
P(1) = −2+16−24 = −10 < 0 ✓
P(7) = −98+112−24 = −10 < 0 ✓
Not quite. Find the zeros of P(t) = 0 first: −2t²+16t−24 = 0 → t²−8t+12 = 0 → (t−2)(t−6) = 0 → t=2 or t=6. Since the parabola opens downward (−2t²), P is positive BETWEEN the roots.
Positive between roots: 2 < t < 6
